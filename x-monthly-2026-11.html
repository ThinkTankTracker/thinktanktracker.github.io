    @ChrisRMcGuireIt is so frustrating and short-sighted that we haven’t updated export controls to stop this. This loophole has been reported for years. Commerce controls high bandwidth memory chips in 3A090.c. But unlike for AI chips, it does not have a corresponding control in 4A090 that also controls items that CONTAIN a high-bandwidth memory chip. This is why a high-bandwidth memory chip in isolation is controlled, but a chip soldered to a board is not. This makes no sense. The fix here is easy: add a new control to 4A090.c that reads, “c. Computers, "electronic assemblies," and "components" containing integrated circuits, any of which meets or exceeds the limits in 3A090.c.” That’s it, problem solved. All shipments of items that contain high-bandwidth memory chips would require a license. Also, if Commerce actually takes that advice, at the same time it should also update the regs so that 3A090.a and 4A090.a also include the memory bandwidth and interconnect parameters that Commerce has privately subjected Nvidia, AMD, and others to since April 2025, but has never made public. Putting these requirements into public regulations and making them applicable to every company could have helped stop some of this smuggling. It’s also very basic good governance, as export controls should wherever possible be transparent and applied uniformly, not private and applied only on certain companies. This is yet another consequence of Commerce not taking export controls seriously. The next time BIS claims they are doing more on export control enforcement than ever, remember this story, the Megaspeed fiasco, and the many others like them that are published every week. Export control enforcement has never been worse.

    @YashengHuangThe Rise of the China Model A widespread notion is that China’s impressive growth was because of “the China Model”—statist finance, massive infrastructural investments, and industrial policy. Yes, the China Model produced impressive GDP growth but in the 1980s, the growth model then—which I call “the directionally-liberal model”—produced equally robust GDP growth, plus comparable household income growth and an economy dependent on domestic consumption. Gini fell and it stayed at a level typical of other East Asian economies. A fundamental feature of this model is that it was pro-rural and because rural economy was more market-driven, a pro-rural orientation was also more pro-market, and pro-people when 70 to 80 percent of the population was rural. In the 1990s, the leadership pivoted away from this model, to a more pro-urban China Model, which is familiar to observers of China. I would venture that the many have very little knowledge of: 1) that a directionally-liberal model ever existed, 2) that it produced the kind of benefits to the Chinese households proportional to the GDP growth, and 3) that there was this consequential pivot in the 1990s that fundamentally changed the nature of the Chinese growth. The goal of my 2026 book is to set the record straight and since the publication of this book, I have now been working on a book project that will present even more detailed data and evidence about this consequential policy pivot and about the long-term economic consequences of this policy pivot. The topics that occupy the current narratives about China, overcapacity, across-the-board export surge, and anemic domestic consumption, can all be traced to their root—this shift in the 1990s from a pro-rural growth model to one that is more pro-urban.
