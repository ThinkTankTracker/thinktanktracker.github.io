    Bloomberg on Russia's version of Dutch disease: "Instead of oil revenues pulling workers and capital into the energy sector while hollowing out the rest of the economy, massive military spending is drawing them into defense industries, pushing up labor costs and steadily eroding the competitiveness of civilian businesses." In a closed economic system, like the global economy, rising wages may be painful for businesses in the short term, but as Michael Kalecki explianed, over the longer term they are not a bad thing. By driving demand for production and by creating incentives for businesses to invest in productivity-enhancing technology, they result in faster production growth that ultimately accommodates not only higher wages but also higher profits. A problem for Russia (and for other countries that try to raise wages) is that it is a part of a badly-globalized economic system, one in which countries are globally competitive in manufacturing mainly to the extent that they are able to suppress wage growth relative to productivity growth. When wages in an economy grow relative the productivity, in other words, that may be good for global demand and for global growth, but it makes that economy less competitive in manufacturing, sending more than 100% of the benefits of higher wages to manufacturing to its trade partners. Russia thus faces the double economic problem not just of an incredibly expensive war, but of rising wages which will further undermine its already-struggling manufacturing sector for the benefit of its trade partners. Russia is not the example that I would have normally used, but it nonetheless illustrates an important point: the global economy would be much better off with a trade and capital regime that rewards economies that raise wages rather than punish them. https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-06/russia-s-war-economy-is-fueling-pay-gains-companies-can-t-afford?srnd=phx-economics-v2post ↗
