    “Could we, and should we, divert some of the massive investments in AI towards public health if what we want is better health and longer lives for Americans? “ something to ponder on….post ↗原帖 ↗
    @DAcemogluMITFourth question on AI. One of the great promises of AI is the discovery of new drugs and cures, so that we can live longer and healthier lives. If and when this becomes a reality, it would indeed be a big achievement. These potential health benefits are often invoked to justify current investments (and lack of regulations). Some even argue that slowing down AI would harm humanity by delaying these benefits. (See https://a16z.com/the-techno-optimist-manifesto/). This raises another uncomfortable question: can we justify rapid and large AI investments based on health benefits? Here is why I think this is an uncomfortable question. First, despite significant effort, AI has so far produced few gains in drug discovery or new cures. A fascinating new paper by Ryan Hill and Carolyn Stein documents significant (downstream) scientific work on proteins whose structures AlphaFold predicted. But the authors conclude “we find no evidence so far that more applied, early-stage drug development is targeting these proteins.” (See their paper here https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6395041). This thought-provoking post by Daphne Koller explains the difficulties that AI is currently facing in drug discovery. Koller emphasizes that AI-based research is focused on the last layer of drug development (creating new molecular entities for already well-understood therapeutic modalities), rather than targeting new disease mechanisms (see https://deepphenotype.substack.com/p/drug-discovery-has-no-magic-wands). Understanding disease mechanisms is harder because there is much less data on it, and this endeavor requires more innovative approaches. It remains an open question whether current AI models are capable of doing this. Second and more importantly, if you want to improve life expectancy and health in the United States, there is plenty of low hanging fruit and diverting some of the huge investments going to AI for this purpose would likely do much more for health outcomes. The United States has the lowest life expectancy at birth among large rich economies. Americans live about five years less, in expectation, than the citizens of Switzerland, Sweden, Japan, Italy, South Korea and several other industrialized countries, and most of the gap comes from deaths before age of 70 due to chronic diseases, overdoses and other preventable causes (see, for example, https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/what-drives-differences-in-life-expectancy-between-the-u-s-and-comparable-countries/). These largely reflect failures in US public health: preventable problems, unhealthy diets, insufficient immunization rates, poor access to primary care; and poor information about health. The country spends much more than other peer economies on healthcare, but too little and too ineffectively on public health (here is a reference to my own work on this: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20231000). Could we, and should we, divert some of the massive investments in AI towards public health if what we want is better health and longer lives for Americans? (And yes, new drugs will benefit citizens of other countries as well, but they can also invest more in public health and the bulk of global AI spending is currently in the United States).

    A quick thread on this latest story on diversion of GPUs to China. 🧵 It is an issue increasingly OBeD and outdated by the developments of the past 6 months, as we have enterted the Age of Agentic Super Intelligence.post ↗原帖 ↗
    @pstAsiatechThe latest diversion story.... Money quote: “US prosecutors allege less than one half of one percent of Nvidia products have been diverted.” @pstAsiatech Repeats a lot of the same old discredited narratives and breaks no new ground https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-01/nvidia-faces-questions-over-china-ai-chip-smuggling-cases?utm_source=website&utm_medium=share&utm_campaign=twitter
    It slides from legitimate compliance questions into an undefined obligation for Nvidia to implement a broader anti-China strategy. Allegedly fake leases, insufficient facilities, and possible employee involvement warrant serious investigation. Existing BIS guidance already requires companies to investigate suspicious circumstances and prohibits deliberate avoidance of relevant information. But the quoted criticism of Nvidia for following the law introduces a different standard: that a company should restrict otherwise permitted business because China is a geopolitical adversary. The article should distinguish failures to satisfy existing compliance duties from demands that Nvidia voluntarily exceed government policy. It also needs clearer attribution among Nvidia, server manufacturers, distributors, cloud operators, individual employees, and BIS. An employee’s alleged participation is serious; it does not by itself establish corporate authorization or knowledge. As I have noted, supply chains involving GPUs are very complicated, there is no real acknowedgment of this here. This Substack should be a must read for anyone writing on the topic, industry sources told me it was the best thing they have read on this complex topic, more than a year ago: https://pstaidecrypted.substack.com/p/us-government-concern-over-gpu-diversionspost ↗原帖 ↗
    And finally, the reporting conflates evidence of leakage with evidence of overall policy failure—and commercial AI competition with demonstrated military harm. Documented smuggling can establish enforcement weaknesses. It cannot, without a counterfactual, establish how much export controls have reduced access, raised costs, delayed deployment, or encouraged substitution. Conversely, China’s continued model progress does not establish that controls have had no effect. The report also moves from military procurement examples to commercial labs’ benchmark performance and then to a sweeping national-security narrative without tracing the intervening links. The vast majority of the uses cases in China for all of the private sector company access to domestic, diverted, or remotely accessed GPUs are civilian! Not military, let's at least acknowledge this reality, instead of making vague allusions to all of this activity somehow contributing to China's military capabilities. The discussion of Huang’s relationship with Trump and their AI-safety rhetoric adds political context, but supplies little evidence about particular diversion transactions and is really irrelevant to the topic at hand. The critical missing analysis is what the leakage changes strategically, relative to unrestricted access—and whether proposed remedies would materially reduce it at an acceptable cost. Along with what I believe is the real issue, how does the US collaborate with China on AI safety, and how can Chinese AI companies test advanced models without access to sufficient compute? That is the real national security question going forward, not bean counting of diverted GPUs without any sense of how they are being used and what difference they make. End Thread.post ↗原帖 ↗
