    @badvaloremIt's really popular to claim that US global tariffs are counterproductive because they alienate our allies and make it harder to create a united front against China. This is wrong for multiple reasons: 1. China has already penetrated global markets. If you tariff China alone, they can just transship or manufacture through third countries. Their overcapacity is so massive that it pushes down global prices, not just Chinese prices. Tariffing China alone therefore does little to address the global impact of Chinese distortions. 2. The U.S. has always been the importer of last resort. If we tariff China, China can just divert exports to other markets in Europe, Asia, and the Americas, which in turn offload the pressure on their producers by increasing exports to the U.S. If we leave our borders open to every country except China, we still end up balancing China's overcapacity by importing more from other countries. 3. Our allies have proven that they are unwilling to act on China unless they feel real consequences. Conferences and diplomacy didn't work. Losing the ability to export duty-free to the U.S., on the other hand, really bites. Meanwhile, Europe et. al have to absorb the full force of China Shock 2.0 without the option of offloading the pressure onto the U.S. The result is that our allies finally have some incentive to wake up on China and start cooperating, which never would have occurred in a business-as-usual climate.

    @JasonBordoffHere’s the White House fact sheet on Venezuela oil with more details. My take: The Venezuela oil deal is being celebrated by some as a win for the US, yet that seems a rather 19th-century perspective. Washington appears to be engineering something like a pseudo-national oil company: a US-influenced entity built to develop a foreign country’s oil, through equity, board control, and offtake rights that echo British control over Persian oil a century ago. With the ousting of Venezuela's president, the interim president’s hold on power depends more on continued US backing than on a mandate from Venezuelans. She now seems to have handed control of a significant share of the country’s oil to a company run by a controversial businessman — a company with a 35% equity stake for the US govt, and with US veto power over its board. There also seem to be inconsistencies with Ven law (e.g., prohibition on concessions, role for PDVSA, etc). There can be a very positive role for the US to play in rebuilding Venezuela’s battered oil industry. But the better path to Venezuela’s stabilization and economic recovery is strengthening its rule of law and governance to build the confidence private capital needs to invest for the long term and bring benefits to the Venezuelan people, rather than opaque deals that hand control to entities substantially owned by a foreign government. I hear a lot of rhetoric in DC, especially from the right, about the benefits of free markets and concern about govt interference, yet engineering a state-influenced oil company to develop foreign energy resources for the US seems like a rather large government role in private enterprise.
