    @petereharrellI have been saying for months that the new USMCA will impose new rules of origin (ROOs) for a range of products, and now the WSJ is reporting the same, starting with AI equipment. Let's unpack: 1. The USMCA today mostly imposes industry-specific rules of origin on autos and on textiles. Autos, for example, must have 75% North American content, and textile "yarn forward" rules mean much apparel has to be made with North American yarn/fabric. ROOs for other products mostly track longstanding rules, including, importantly, the "substantial transformation" principle that allows many products assembled in Mexico/Canada from foreign parts to qualify for USCMA tariff treatment. 2. The Trump Administration--and many Democrats--favor expanding industry specific ROOs to reduce Chinese parts and increase North American content other products. The WSJ says that USTR wants new ROOs for AI products, which could mean, for example, that AI servers have to include North American (really, US) chips and various other North American content. The WSJ reports the Admin is also looking at new ROOs for "a litany of consumer products" including "medical equipment." 3. The challenge with new ROOs is two-fold. First, companies have to figure out how to comply: historically many companies that assemble products in Mexico were not rigorously tracking the origin of parts. Many are now beginning to do so, on the assumption that they will need to know this to keep getting USMCA tariff treatment. But industry will have to invest in figuring this out. 4. Second, the US and Mexico (and hopefully Canada) will have to figure what they want the ROOs to be. For the USMCA, a "regional content" requirement would mean that a minimum percentage of parts has to come from the US, Mexico, or Canada. But the reality is that plenty of items currently assembled in Mexico have lots of non-North American content, and onshoring all of that content will take time/may not be practical. 5. In theory, the US could try to set a cap on Chinese content, e.g., to qualify for USMCA, a good has to be "substantially transformed" AND have *no more than,* e.g., 20% Chinese content, but European, Taiwanese, etc. content would be OK. Or, potentially, the US could carve in content from US trade deal partners, including Trump "ART" trade deal partners. E.g., to qualify for USMCA treatment, a product has to have minimum content from USMCA trade deal countries OR other US FTA/ART partners. Or of course the US could just set minimum USMCA content requirements and expect component production to move to North America. 6. I will be interested to see exactly how many products USTR seeks to apply new ROOs to/how widely the new ROOs sweep. 7. My strong sense is that USTR plans to nail down new ROOs in the USMCA agreement, and then take the concepts established in those ROOs (product coverage, minimum content requirements, etc.) and try to negotiate similar ROOs with other US trade partners.

When a father wrestles, chases, or tumbles with a child on the floor, the child is learning something specific: how to be excited without losing control.     @jietangTwo weeks. That's how long it took to go from GLM-5.3-Flash's first run on domestic accelerators to serving all of its production traffic, with 3.2× end-to-end throughput along the way. What I keep thinking about is who did much of the work: an Infra Agent powered by GLM-5.3. A model helping optimize the system that serves it. The conditions were hard. Limited memory and interconnect bandwidth. 1M-token context. Multimodal requests. An immature software stack where kernels were missing and documentation was often guesswork. Every optimization was a trade: compute for memory (ReplaySSM), communication for memory (intra-node tensor parallelism), precision for capacity (mixed INT8/FP8/BF16 caching), and disaggregation for scheduling freedom (Encode–Prefill–Decode). But the most important lesson wasn't about any single optimization. When the agent got stuck, it was rarely because it couldn't write the code. It was because it didn't know *why* things got worse. "Throughput down 20%" tells you something broke. It doesn't tell you which layer, which hypothesis, or what to test next. In RL terms, it's a sparse reward with a credit assignment problem. And an end-to-end benchmark that takes hours makes exploration painfully slow. Senior engineers solve this with an implicit process reward in their heads.
